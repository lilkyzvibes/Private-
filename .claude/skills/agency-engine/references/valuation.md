# What the company is actually worth

The owner's stated goal is a company worth millions or billions. Here is the
honest map, because the difference between those two numbers is not effort — it's
a structural change in what the business *is*.

## How services businesses are priced

Small agencies trade on **EBITDA multiples**, roughly:

| Profile | Multiple | On $1M EBITDA |
|---|---|---|
| Owner-dependent, project revenue, concentrated clients | 2–3× | $2–3M |
| Some recurring revenue, owner still in delivery | 3–4× | $3–4M |
| Mostly retainers, owner out of delivery, clean books | 4–6× | $4–6M |
| Retainers, diversified, management team, documented systems | 6–8× | $6–8M |
| Proprietary technology/data doing real work, software-like margins | 8–15×+ | $8–15M+ |

Two agencies with identical revenue and identical profit routinely sell for a 3×
difference in price. The gap is entirely made of the drivers below.

## The five drivers, in order of leverage

**1. Owner independence.** The single biggest one. A buyer is asking: if the
founder leaves, what happens? If the answer is "revenue stops," you don't have a
company, you have a job with a customer list, and it prices accordingly. Getting
the owner out of delivery, then out of sales, is worth more than a year of growth.

**2. Recurring revenue.** Retainers with auto-renew are worth multiples of the
same dollars in project work. Project revenue resets to zero every January; a
retainer book compounds. Push every project client onto a retainer, and offer the
annual prepay — contracted forward revenue is the most legible thing on the page.

**3. Client concentration.** Any client above 20% of revenue is a discount on the
whole business; above 40% and buyers walk. This is why the concentration cap gets
enforced early, when refusing to over-serve your biggest client costs you real
money. It's a valuation decision disguised as a sales decision.

**4. Gross margin.** 60%+ says you're selling judgment. 30% says you're selling
pass-through labor and any buyer can replicate you. The margin ladder in SKILL.md
is the path here.

**5. Documented systems.** Everything in this skill — playbooks, contracts,
scripts, state — is a transferable asset. "Here's how the company runs" turns
tacit knowledge into something a buyer can underwrite. This is the least
glamorous driver and the one most people skip.

## Who buys these

- **Larger agencies and holding companies** buying capability, client relationships,
  or a specific vertical. Typically 4–6×, often with an earnout tying the owner in
  for 2–3 years.
- **Private equity roll-ups** consolidating fragmented agency markets. They pay for
  clean books, recurring revenue, and management depth, and they'll pass instantly
  on owner dependence.
- **Strategic buyers** — a martech or platform company buying a services arm.
  Rarer, sometimes the highest price.

## The honest part about "billions"

A creative talent brokerage does not become a billion-dollar company. The math
doesn't allow it: revenue scales roughly linearly with headcount and clients, and
services multiples are what they are. A world-class outcome for the business
described in this skill is **$5–30M**, which is a genuinely excellent result and
life-changing money.

Getting to a fundamentally larger number requires becoming a different kind of
company — and there is exactly one credible path from here:

## Agency → platform

The agency generates something no software startup can buy: **proprietary data on
which freelancer actually delivers.** Not portfolios, which are marketing — real
performance history. Who hit deadlines, whose work needed redos, who performed on
which brief type, for which client profile, at what rate.

After a few hundred engagements that dataset does something valuable: it lets you
match a brief to the right person faster and more accurately than a human
account manager can. At that point the sequence is:

1. **Instrument the agency.** Log every engagement's brief type, contractor,
   turnaround, revisions, outcome. This costs almost nothing now and cannot be
   reconstructed later. Start immediately, years before you need it.
2. **Productize.** Fixed scopes, fixed prices, standard SLAs. Rung 3. A buyer can
   understand a product; they can't underwrite bespoke.
3. **Automate the matching.** The software does what the account manager did.
   Margin goes up and, critically, revenue stops scaling with headcount.
4. **Open the marketplace.** Clients self-serve against the vetted, scored roster.
   Now you're a platform with a data moat, priced like software.

That path is real — it's roughly how Toptal and several others got built — and
it's long. The important thing is that step 1 is free and starts today. The
agency is how you fund the platform and, more valuably, how you acquire the
dataset the platform needs. Skip the agency and you're a marketplace with no
supply and no data, which is the graveyard.

## Exit readiness — start these three years out

- Clean, reviewed financials, accrual basis. Personal expenses out of the business.
- Every client on a signed MSA with assignable terms. An unassignable contract is
  worth zero in a sale.
- Every contractor on a signed agreement with a valid IP assignment. A broken IP
  chain found in diligence re-prices or kills the deal.
- Concentration under 20%, ideally under 15%.
- A management team that runs the business for 30 days without the owner —
  demonstrably, because a buyer will ask for the evidence.
