# Legal and risk

Not legal advice, and the templates in `assets/` are drafting starting points.
Have a lawyer review the full stack once before first use — it costs a few hundred
dollars and it is the cheapest insurance in the business. After that, reuse freely.

## The contract stack

Four documents. Every engagement uses all four, and the order matters.

| Document | Between | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Client MSA** (`assets/client-msa.md`) | you ↔ client | Master terms. Signed once, covers all future work. |
| **SOW** (`assets/sow-template.md`) | you ↔ client | Per-engagement scope, price, dates. Attaches to the MSA. |
| **Contractor agreement** (`assets/contractor-agreement.md`) | you ↔ freelancer | IP assignment, confidentiality, non-circumvention, payment terms. |
| **Portfolio release** (`assets/portfolio-release.md`) | you ↔ freelancer | Permission to show their work in pitches, with attribution. |

## The IP chain — get this right or you have nothing to sell

You cannot give the client rights you never acquired. The chain must be unbroken:

```
Freelancer  --assigns IP-->  You  --assigns/licenses IP-->  Client
```

If the contractor agreement isn't signed *before* work starts, the freelancer owns
the copyright by default in most jurisdictions, and the rights you just promised
the client in the MSA are rights you don't have. That's a breach of your own
contract and it surfaces at the worst possible moment — during a client's
acquisition due diligence, or when they want to run the work as a paid ad.

**Rule: no brief goes out before the contractor agreement is signed.** No
exceptions for urgency; urgency is exactly when this gets skipped.

Third-party assets are the other half of the chain. Fonts, stock, music, and
images all need licenses covering the client's actual use — commercial and paid
media, not editorial-only. Contractors get this wrong constantly and in good
faith. It's a QA checklist item for a reason.

## Worker classification

Your freelancers must genuinely be independent contractors, not employees wearing
a different label. Misclassification means back taxes, penalties, and in some
states personal liability. The tests vary (the US federal test, California's ABC
test, the UK's IR35, and others) but they converge on the same substance:

**Points toward contractor** — they control how and when work is done; they use
their own equipment; they work for other clients; the engagement is
project-based; they can subcontract; they're paid per project or per invoice.

**Points toward employee** — you set their hours; you require exclusivity; you
supply equipment; it's open-ended and full-time; they're integrated into your
team structure; you're their only source of income.

The practical guidance: **brief outcomes, not hours.** Say what the deliverable is
and when it's due; don't dictate a 9-to-5, don't require exclusivity, and never
tell a contractor they can't take other clients. This is also better management —
the thing that keeps you compliant is the thing that keeps good freelancers
available.

If a contractor is billing you full-time for many months and works for nobody
else, that relationship is drifting toward employment. Get advice before it's a
year old.

## Non-circumvention

12 months, mutual, applying to clients you introduced. It's a real clause and
worth having, but understand what it is: a **deterrent**, not a defense. Suing a
freelancer over a $6k/month client costs more than the client is worth, and
everyone involved knows it.

What actually prevents disintermediation is being necessary — see `delivery.md`.
The clause raises the friction; the value-add removes the motive.

## Hard lines

These are not risk-management preferences. They're the difference between a
company and a liability, and the skill will not help build around them:

- **No ghost job postings.** Listings are for work that exists or a bench you are
  genuinely building and will genuinely hire from. Deceptive postings are an FTC
  §5 problem, a flat platform ToS violation that ends the account, and the subject
  of active state legislation.
- **No presenting someone else's work as your own.** Copyright infringement, false
  advertising under Lanham Act §43(a), and fraudulent inducement of the contract
  the client signed on the strength of it. Permission plus attribution costs one
  signature at onboarding.
- **No unpaid "test projects" used as free labor.** Pay for tests. Small and real.
- **No fabricated clients, case studies, testimonials, or headcount.** It survives
  exactly until the first reference call.

Each of these is also, separately, bad business: they trade a durable asset —
roster trust, client trust, a clean IP chain — for one month of revenue.

## Insurance and admin

Once past ~$250k revenue: professional liability (E&O) at $1–2M, general liability
if you ever set foot on a client site, and an entity (LLC or equivalent) with a
genuinely separate bank account. Clients above a certain size will ask for
certificates of insurance before signing; not having them costs you deals you'll
never hear about.

Keep signed contracts, W-9/W-8BENs, and invoices in one organized place from day
one. It's tedious for three years and then it's the difference between a clean
due diligence and a discounted sale price.
