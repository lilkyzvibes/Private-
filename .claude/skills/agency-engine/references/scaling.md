# Scaling: stage gates and what breaks

Growth in this business is not smooth. There are four points where the thing that
got you here stops working, and each one requires the owner to give something up.
Recognizing which gate you're at is most of the skill.

## Stage 0 → $10k/month · "Can I sell this at all?"

**The job**: find the first three clients. Nothing else matters.

**What breaks**: nothing yet, except the owner's patience. The failure mode is
spending weeks on branding, a website, a logo, and a business plan instead of
sending ten messages a day. None of that has ever closed a retainer.

**Do**: 10 targeted contacts daily, one channel only (stale reqs), one craft only.
Deliver the first engagements yourself if you can, or with two freelancers you
already trust.

**Gate**: three retainers, all above the 50% margin floor.

## $10k → $50k/month · "Can it run without me doing the work?"

**The job**: get the owner out of *producing* and into briefing, QA, and selling.

**What breaks**: the roster. Two freelancers cannot cover six clients, and the
first time one goes quiet mid-engagement you'll understand why depth matters.
Also: briefs get sloppy under volume, and redos start eating margin.

**Do**: get to 3-deep on your top two crafts. Write the brief template and use it
every time. Add the second demand channel. Start logging engagement outcomes per
contractor — this is the beginning of the dataset that matters later.

**Gate**: owner spends zero hours producing. Blended margin still ≥55%.

## $50k → $150k/month · "Can it run without me managing?"

**The job**: hire the first employee. This is the hardest gate and where most
brokerages stall permanently, because the owner is simultaneously the only
salesperson and the only account manager, and both are full-time.

**What breaks**: the owner. Twelve to twenty clients is exactly the point where
delivery oversight consumes the hours that used to go to selling, so new business
stops, so the business plateaus at the owner's personal capacity — which is the
definition of a job.

**Do**: hire a **project manager first**, not a salesperson. Counterintuitive but
correct: the PM buys back the owner's delivery hours, and the owner is still the
best salesperson in the company by a wide margin. Selling is the last thing to
delegate, not the first. Document delivery properly — `delivery.md` becomes the
PM's manual.

**Gate**: owner spends <20% of time on delivery. Concentration under 25%.

## $150k/month → platform · "Can it stop scaling with headcount?"

**The job**: productize, then automate the matching. See `valuation.md`.

**What breaks**: linearity. Every new client needs a new slice of an account
manager, so revenue and cost grow together and margin stops improving. That's the
ceiling of the services model, and no amount of effort moves it.

**Do**: standardize offers into fixed-scope products. Build the matching layer on
the engagement data you've been logging since stage 2. Move clients to self-serve
where it fits.

**Gate**: revenue per employee rising instead of flat.

## The failure modes, by frequency

**Stalling at the owner's capacity.** By far the most common. Symptom: revenue
flat for two quarters while the owner is busier than ever. Cause: refusing to
hire the PM. The business is fine; the owner is the bottleneck and won't admit it.

**Concentration.** One client grows to 50% of revenue because serving them is
easier than finding three more. Then they leave, or just renegotiate, and the
year is gone. Cap it at 20% while it still costs you something.

**Margin drift.** Nobody decides to work for 35% margin; it happens one unpriced
favor at a time. Run `economics.py pnl` monthly and act on the trend, not the
level.

**Selling more than the roster can deliver.** Closing a client you can't staff
within 48 hours converts a win into a reputation problem. Roster depth is a
leading indicator of sellable capacity — grow it *before* the demand.

**Hiring a salesperson too early.** They need a proven, documented process to
sell into, and at stage 2 you don't have one — you have the owner's intuition.
They'll fail, and it'll cost six months and a salary.

## The weekly rhythm, at every stage

Monday: pipeline review, coverage check, plan the week's outreach.
Daily: 10 targeted contacts, minimum, regardless of how busy delivery is.
Wednesday: delivery checkpoints across live engagements.
Friday: invoices out, state updated, margins reviewed, next week's briefs written.

The daily contact quota is the one thing that never gets skipped. Every stall in
this business traces back to a week where delivery felt urgent and demand felt
optional.
