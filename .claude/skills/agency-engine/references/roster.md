# Roster: sourcing, vetting, tiers, onboarding

The roster is the asset. Clients churn, channels dry up, but a bench of 40 vetted
specialists with performance history is what makes the company worth a multiple
rather than a salary. Treat it as the balance sheet.

## Sourcing

Post real calls for a real bench. The listing is honest about what it is —
project work as it lands, not a salaried role — and that honesty is why good
people answer it.

> **Building a vetted bench — senior motion designers (remote, project-based)**
>
> We place senior creative talent on client retainers. This is a roster call, not
> a staff role: you'd be added to our bench and offered paid project work as it
> comes in, at $X–Y/hr or per-project rates. No exclusivity, no obligation to take
> anything.
>
> We'll ask for your portfolio and a short paid test project ($150) before adding
> you. If we show your work in a client pitch, we ask permission and credit you.
>
> Apply: {link}

Every line of that does work. "Roster call, not a staff role" prevents the
resentment that kills referrals. Naming the rate filters out everyone who'd waste
your time. The paid test signals you're not harvesting free labor. The permission
line is the promise that gets you talked about positively in freelancer
communities — which is where the next fifty applicants come from.

**Where to post**: Working Not Working, Dribbble, Behance, r/forhire, craft-specific
Discords and Slack groups, Contra, and your existing roster's referrals. Referrals
from a happy bench are the highest-quality source by a distance; pay a $250 bonus
when a referral clears the test project.

## Vetting rubric

Score every applicant out of 20. Anything under 14 is a no, and hold that line —
a weak roster addition costs you a client, and a client costs you ten times what
the freelancer would have earned you.

| Dimension | Weight | What you're actually checking |
|---|---|---|
| Craft quality | 5 | Best three pieces in the relevant craft. Not range — depth in the one thing you'd hire them for. |
| Relevance | 4 | Have they done work in this exact context (DTC ads, SaaS brand, editorial)? Adjacent-but-different is a 2. |
| Responsiveness | 4 | Time to reply during application. This predicts delivery behavior more reliably than portfolio quality. |
| Test project | 5 | Paid, small, real. Judge brief adherence and turnaround as much as output. |
| Professionalism | 2 | Clear rates, clean communication, asks good questions about the brief. |

**Always pay for the test project.** $100–200. It's the single best money you
spend: it filters out anyone not serious, it's the only reliable predictor of how
they handle a real brief, and unpaid tests are how you become the thing the video
described. Word travels.

**Responsiveness is the sleeper variable.** A brilliant designer who goes quiet
for three days will lose you a client; a good designer who replies in two hours
will keep one for years. Weight it accordingly and re-check it every engagement.

## Tiers

| Tier | Who | Your cost | Bill at | Use for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Proven on 3+ engagements, no incidents | $75–120/hr | 2.5–3× | Flagship clients, anything new or risky |
| 2 | Passed vetting, 1–2 engagements clean | $50–75/hr | 3× | The default working tier |
| 3 | Passed vetting, unproven | $35–55/hr | 3–3.5× | Volume work, overflow, low-risk briefs |

Promote on delivered performance, never on tenure. Demote or remove after two
incidents — a missed deadline without warning, or work that needed a full redo.
Removal is quiet and polite; they may come back better, and they will definitely
talk about how you handled it.

## Rates and margin

Your billing multiple on contractor cost should be **2.5–3.5×**. Below 2.5× you
cannot absorb a redo, and redos happen. Above 3.5× on rung-1 pass-through work you
are exposed to disintermediation the moment anyone compares notes — earn the top
of the range by actually adding the management layer.

Pay **net 30 after client acceptance**, and say so plainly in the agreement so
nobody is surprised. Never pay before the client has paid you; that inverts the
cash cycle and is how brokerages die. The one exception worth making: for a Tier 1
contractor on a long engagement, paying on your own schedule regardless of client
delay buys loyalty that's worth more than the float. Make that a deliberate
decision, not a drift.

## Onboarding

Get all of this before the first brief, because chasing paperwork mid-engagement
is how IP chains break:

1. **Contractor agreement signed** — `assets/contractor-agreement.md`. Contains
   the IP assignment and non-circumvention.
2. **Portfolio release signed** — `assets/portfolio-release.md`. This is what makes
   it legal to show their work in a pitch. Get it at onboarding, when it's a
   formality, not when you need it at 11pm before a call.
3. **W-9 / W-8BEN** (US / non-US) for tax reporting.
4. **Payment details** and their stated rate, confirmed in writing.
5. **Recorded in state**: `pipeline.py add-talent --name ... --craft ... --rate ... --tier ...`

## Keeping the bench warm

An unpaid bench is a fiction — people take full-time jobs and don't tell you. Two
cheap habits keep it real:

- **Quarterly ping**: one message asking about availability and current rate.
  Costs you an hour, prevents the discovery mid-crisis that your best editor went
  in-house in March.
- **Distribute work deliberately.** Don't give everything to your favorite. Three
  people billing a little each stay available; one person billing everything
  becomes a single point of failure *and* your most likely disintermediation risk.

## Depth targets

- Top two crafts: **3 deep minimum** before widening into a third craft.
- Every craft you actively sell: at least 2, one of them Tier 1 or 2.
- If you can't staff a signed brief within 48 hours, the roster is too thin for
  what you're selling. Fix the roster before selling more of it.
