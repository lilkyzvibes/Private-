# Delivery: brief, produce, QA, present

Delivery is where the margin either holds or evaporates, and it's where
disintermediation is either prevented or invited. The structural rule underneath
everything here: **you own the brief, the QA, and the client conversation.** The
contractor produces. That division is what makes you necessary rather than
decorative.

## The brief

Bad briefs are the single largest cost in this business. A vague brief produces a
redo, a redo eats your margin, and two redos lose the client. Ten minutes writing
a real brief saves a day.

Every brief goes to the contractor in writing with all seven of these:

1. **Client and context** — who they are, who they sell to, what this is for.
2. **Deliverable** — exact quantity, format, dimensions, duration, file types.
3. **Reference** — 2–3 examples of the target, and at least one example of what to
   avoid. The negative example is worth more than the positive ones.
4. **Constraints** — brand guidelines, mandatory elements, legal or claims limits.
5. **Deadline** — your internal deadline, set **2 days before** the client's.
   That buffer is not optional; it's the difference between a QA pass and an
   apology.
6. **Revision rounds included** — name the number so it isn't a negotiation later.
7. **Rate and total** — agreed in writing before work starts, every time.

## Cadence

- **Kickoff** — brief delivered, contractor confirms understanding *in their own
  words*. If the confirmation is just "got it," ask what they're going to make.
  Misunderstandings surface here for free and nowhere else for free.
- **Checkpoint at 30%** — for anything over a week. One rough version, early. This
  single habit prevents most disasters; a wrong direction caught at 30% costs a
  day, at 100% it costs the engagement.
- **Internal delivery** — 2 days before the client deadline.
- **QA pass** — yours (below).
- **Client delivery** — from you, presented, never a forwarded file.

## QA

Nothing reaches the client without passing this. Every item, every time — the one
you skip is the one that goes out wrong.

- Brief adherence: does it do what was actually asked?
- Specs: dimensions, duration, format, file naming, deliverable count.
- Brand: correct logo, colors, fonts, legal marks.
- Copy: spelling, client's product names spelled the client's way, no placeholder
  text, no lorem ipsum in a corner.
- Rights: every asset — font, stock, music, image — licensed for the client's
  actual use. Commercial and paid-media use, not just editorial. This is the one
  that generates real legal letters.
- Fresh eyes: would you send this to a client who's paying $12k/month?

If it fails, it goes back to the contractor with specifics — never fix it
yourself. Fixing it yourself hides the problem, costs your time, and teaches them
nothing. That's what the 2-day buffer is for.

## Presenting

Always present, never forward. A file dropped in Slack is a commodity; a short
walk-through is a service, and it's the thing they're paying the margin for.

Frame every delivery against the brief: *"You asked for 20 concepts against the
three angles that are working. Here they are grouped by angle — these four are
the strongest and here's why."* You are showing judgment, which is the product.

Handle revisions in one consolidated round. Scattered one-off requests destroy
margin and train the client to treat you as an inbox. "Send me everything by
Thursday and we'll turn the full round Monday."

## Account management

- **Weekly**: a short written update per client — shipped, in progress, needed
  from them. Five minutes, and it's most of why clients renew.
- **Monthly**: a brief review — what shipped, what performed, what's next month.
  This is also where expansion happens naturally; a client seeing volume
  delivered is a client who'll add a second craft.
- **Quarterly**: the honest conversation. Is this working? Anything you'd change?
  Ask it before they're thinking it, and you'll usually save the account.

## Scope creep

Say yes to the work and yes to the invoice. The failure mode isn't refusing the
extra work — it's doing it free and resenting it.

> "Happy to take that on. It's outside the current scope, so it'd be an extra
> {$X} this month or we can swap it against {existing deliverable} — either
> works, tell me which you prefer."

Neutral, immediate, and it gives them a free option. Handled this way, scope
creep is an upsell channel rather than a margin leak. Handled by silence, it's
how a 60% engagement quietly becomes a 35% one.

## When it goes wrong

Something will go badly wrong roughly once a quarter. How you handle it is more
visible to the client than a year of things going right.

1. **Tell the client before they find out.** Always. Discovery-by-client is what
   loses accounts; a proactive heads-up rarely does.
2. **Own it without naming the contractor.** "We missed this" — not "our freelancer
   missed this." You sold accountability; deliver it. Naming the contractor tells
   the client your value-add is imaginary and invites them to go direct.
3. **Fix it fast and at your cost.** Escalate to a Tier 1 contractor and eat the
   margin on that deliverable. One bad month at 20% margin beats a churned client.
4. **Say what changes.** A concrete process change — earlier checkpoint, different
   contractor, extra QA step — converts the incident into evidence you're managed.
5. **Record it in state** against that contractor. Two incidents means removal.

Only offer a credit if you actually failed to deliver something billed. Reflexive
discounting after every hiccup teaches the client to find hiccups.
